\chapter{CONTROL AND SYSTEM INTELLIGENCE}
\label{chap:control}

\section{Overview}
\label{sec:o6-1}

This chapter presents the main technical contribution of the project. All of it runs in the microcontroller firmware, with the single exception of the rolling baseline leak detector of Section~\ref{sec:o6-6}, which sits on the backend for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:o3-2}. The control loop runs on a fixed 200 millisecond cycle of six steps: read the sensors, filter the level, update the parallel level model, evaluate the fault rules, run the state machine, and drive the outputs. The loop does not depend on network state; the only network work inside it is bounded as described in Section~\ref{sec:o4-4}.

\section{Computed quantities}
\label{sec:o6-2}

\textbf{Level.} The ultrasonic sensor measures the distance $d$ from the transducer to the water, and the level is $h = 15.88 - d$ centimetres, then corrected as $h_{corr} = a \cdot h + b$ with coefficients from the calibration of Section~\ref{sec:o8-2}. Because a share of echoes in this narrow tank return from the wall or the floor (Section~\ref{sec:o3-3}), a single ping is never trusted. Each 200 millisecond cycle fires one ping and the reading passes through a chain of gates, each of which counts its own rejections so that the chain can be tuned from evidence rather than intuition:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Window and geometry.} The last fifteen pings are kept; pings outside the physical geometry of the tank are discarded, in particular any distance more than 1 centimetre beyond the floor, which only a stray echo can produce.
\item \textbf{Spread.} If the interquartile range of the window exceeds 3.5 centimetres the window is rejected, because the sensor is then alternating between two surfaces and no single statistic of the window means anything. Appendix \ref{app:bench} explains why this is the interquartile range and not the full range.
\item \textbf{Lower quartile.} The 25th percentile of the window becomes the candidate reading. Like the median it tolerates isolated outliers that would drag a mean, and it adds a deliberate bias towards the shorter distance: a weak echo is detected one or more 40 kilohertz cycles late, about 0.43 centimetres per cycle, so errors in this tank only ever lengthen the distance, and the shorter readings are the trustworthy ones.
\item \textbf{Rate.} The candidate is rejected if it differs from the last accepted value by more than physics allows in the elapsed time plus a 2 centimetre noise allowance, and, while the pump runs, if it lies more than 1 centimetre below the filtered level, since the tank cannot drain faster than a running pump fills it. An earlier version compared against the last accepted reading, and after one rejection that reference could never catch up, so the gate locked itself shut.
\item \textbf{Alpha-beta filter.} Accepted readings feed a two state filter that tracks both level $\hat{h}$ and its rate of change $\hat{v}$:
\begin{equation}
\hat{h}^- = \hat{h} + \hat{v}\,\Delta t,\quad r = h - \hat{h}^-,\quad
\hat{h} \leftarrow \hat{h}^- + \alpha r,\quad \hat{v} \leftarrow \hat{v} + \frac{\beta}{\Delta t} r
\label{eq:alphabeta}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha = 0.03$ and $\beta = \alpha^2/(2-\alpha)$. The earlier exponential moving average lagged a rising level by a constant offset; the alpha-beta filter tracks a steady ramp without lag, and its rate estimate $\hat{v}$ is used below for flow and for pump diagnosis.
\end{enumerate}

If no reading is accepted for 5 seconds the level is marked untrusted (\texttt{level\_ok} false). Independently of all of the above, a raw distance guard reads the unfiltered distance on every ping and declares the water too close after three consecutive readings nearer than 4.5 centimetres to the transducer. That guard shares nothing with the filter chain, so a filter defect cannot disable it.

\textbf{Parallel level model.} During a fill the sensor sometimes produces no accepted reading for tens of seconds, and the earliest firmware stopped every such fill at about half the target. The firmware now runs a model of the tank in parallel with the measurement, integrating $dh/dt = (Q_{in} - Q_{out})/A$ and pulling the model towards each accepted reading with a gain of 0.05. The inflow $Q_{in}$ is the inlet turbine reading whenever it exceeds 0.05 litres per minute; otherwise it is the calibrated pump delivery while the pump runs and is not labelled \texttt{no\_flow}, and zero when it is stopped. $Q_{out}$ is the latest drain estimate. Feeding the model the measured inflow matters because the pump delivery changes with the level of the source bucket, from 0.36 to 0.24 litres per minute on different days, and a model that drifts cannot be used to judge whether a new reading is believable.

While the level is untrusted the state machine controls on the model. This is how a fill crosses the blind zone of Section~\ref{sec:o3-3}, which takes 40 to 75 seconds at bench flow rates. The model is never allowed to replace the sensor for long: after 90 seconds without a single accepted reading the pump stops and \texttt{LEVEL\_LOST} is raised, and a returning reading that disagrees with the model by more than 2.5 centimetres is rejected rather than adopted. A previous revision accepted such a reading after it had repeated for several cycles, on the argument that a value that keeps returning is real. The blind zone refuted that argument: its stray echo is perfectly repeatable, and the rule let it through as a level of 0 percent, which started the pump every 40 seconds. That acceptance path is now disabled. The overflow float, the raw distance guard and the run time limits keep working throughout any blind interval.

\textbf{Flow.} Section~\ref{sec:o3-3} explains why the turbine readings are not used in this installation. With flow derived from level, the reported inlet flow and the pumped volume use the calibrated pump delivery of 0.36 litres per minute while the pump runs and zero otherwise; the turbine reading is published beside it for comparison but does not yet feed the volume counter. Outlet flow is computed from the rate estimate as $Q_{out} = -\hat{v} \cdot A \cdot 60/1000$ litres per minute, but only when the pump has been off for at least 20 seconds and the surface has settled; the estimate is smoothed and then \emph{held} while the pump runs. An earlier version computed outlet flow as pump delivery minus the net level rate at every instant. Because the delivery term switches instantly while the rate estimate lags, every pump start made the outlet curve jump by the full pump delivery and decay back, which on the dashboard looked as if consumption rose whenever the pump started. Holding the drain estimate removed the artefact and matches the physics: the valve setting, not the pump, determines the draw.

\textbf{Volume.} Accumulated volume is the numerical integral of flow,
\begin{equation}
V(t) = \sum_{i} Q_i \cdot \frac{\Delta t}{60},
\label{eq:volume}
\end{equation}
computed on the microcontroller rather than the server, because if it ran on the server every message lost during an outage would become water permanently unaccounted for. The totals are written to non volatile storage every 60 seconds, so a sudden power loss costs at most one minute. Section~\ref{sec:o8-2} measures the resulting volume error at $-5$ percent on average.

\textbf{Current.} Pump current is reported in milliamperes using the scale factor of Section~\ref{sec:o3-3}, with readings under the 4 millivolt noise floor reported as zero.

\label{sec:o6-3}

The reason for a state machine rather than a plain conditional expression is that fault states need to be sticky. A conditional rule would allow the pump to restart the moment a transient fault condition disappears, for instance when the suction tube touches the water again; the state machine holds the system in the fault state until an explicit intervention occurs. One invariant is enforced at the top of every cycle: whenever a fault code is set, the state must be a fault state. An early version let the manual and filling states return to idle without clearing the fault code, which left a device that looked idle and healthy while silently refusing every start.

The transition between \texttt{IDLE} and \texttt{FILLING} implements equation \eqref{eq:hysteresis} with thresholds of 30 and 70 percent, leaving a 15 percent margin up to the 85 percent safety limit to absorb overshoot. Two timing constraints complement the band: a minimum on time of 3 seconds and a minimum off time of 20 seconds. Hysteresis rejects measurement noise near the threshold, while the timing constraints reject genuine short period oscillations and protect the pump from repeated inrush.

The minimum on time deserves a note, because the first draft set it to 10 seconds and that value was unsafe. At the measured 0.36 litres per minute a forced 10 second run adds 0.06 litres, nearly 5 percent of this 1.24 litre tank, while the state machine has no authority to stop it. Three seconds is long enough to stop the relay chattering and short enough that the band still governs. A hysteresis width of 40 percent may look wide for a control system, but the tank is a buffer: the goal is to guarantee water availability while limiting pump starts, not to hold a set point.

\section{Fault detection rules}
\label{sec:o6-4}

The system implements eleven rules where the assignment asks for at least one. They are evaluated every cycle in priority order and the first rule that fires ends the chain. Firing a rule stops the pump, lights the fault lamp, and publishes a fault message.

\texttt{SENSOR\_CONFLICT} is a direct application of the independent source consistency principle of Section~\ref{sec:o2-6} \cite{isermann2006}. With only an ultrasonic sensor the system cannot tell a right reading from a wrong one as long as the value stays plausible; a mechanical float turns a wrong reading into a detectable contradiction.

The assignment's example rule, pump on with near zero flow, appears twice. \texttt{DRY\_RUN} detects it from the pump current. A submerged pump pushing water draws 320 to 330 milliamperes; the same pump lifted out of the water draws 215 to 230, because the motor runs without load (measured on 7 October). The rule fires when current is present but below 270 milliamperes for three seconds after the start transient, so a dry pump is stopped within about five seconds instead of ninety. \texttt{NO\_PROGRESS} implements the same physical idea on a second, independent quantity: a pump that draws current while the level does not rise is not moving water. Its mark only ever moves down with noise and moves up only on real progress, so a single high outlier cannot hide a stalled fill. Appendix \ref{app:bench} records a bench episode in which the level reading was false for the whole of a fill and this rule, which never consults the validity flag, still stopped the pump.

The sensor timeout is long by design. The ultrasonic sensor in this tank drops out for tens of seconds during normal fills, and crossing the blind zone takes up to 75 seconds; the parallel model bridges those gaps, and a short timeout would stop every fill early, which the first version did at 4 seconds and the 45 second version still did inside the blind zone. The limit is therefore 90 seconds while the model is valid and 45 seconds otherwise. The price is that a disconnected sensor is reported after up to 90 seconds rather than 4, which is acceptable because every overflow layer that does not rely on the sensor remains active meanwhile.

\section{Manual mode and safety interlocks}
\label{sec:o6-5}

The assignment requires that manual mode must not override safety constraints. The group meets this with an architectural decision inside the firmware: \textbf{every code path capable of starting the pump is forced through one single guard function.} The function returns nothing when starting is permitted and a reason code when it is blocked, checking in order: an active fault, the upper float, the raw distance guard, level at or above the safety limit, an untrusted level, and the minimum off time.

An earlier revision listed an empty source tank as a further condition. It was removed, and the removal is worth recording: the source vessel carries no instrument, and the condition was in fact reading the lower float of the \emph{storage} tank under a wrong label. Blocking the pump on a quantity the system cannot observe is not a safety measure but a permanent refusal to operate, and dry running is covered by rules that rest on quantities the system does observe: absent current and absent level progress.

Because both the automatic state machine and user commands pass through the same guard, verifying the interlock means reviewing one function rather than auditing the codebase. When a command is refused, the acknowledgement carries the reason. Moreover, while in \texttt{MANUAL\_ON}, the guard is re-evaluated on every cycle, so lifting the upper float stops a manually started pump within one 200 millisecond cycle, and the fault cannot be cleared while the float is still up: the clear command is answered with \texttt{condition\_still\_present}. One exception keeps manual and automatic filling consistent: a pump that is already running is not stopped merely because the level reading has just become untrusted, for instance in the blind zone of Section~\ref{sec:o3-3}. It continues on the tank model for at most 90 seconds, with the overflow limit applied to the model level. A manual start is also accepted while the reading is untrusted, as long as the model is valid; otherwise water standing still inside the blind zone would make manual pumping impossible. Each such start may again run blind for at most 90 seconds, and the float and the raw distance guard still apply.

\section{Leak and anomalous consumption detection}
\label{sec:o6-6}

This is one of the two advanced options in the assignment. It is built as two mechanisms at two places in the system.

The edge mechanism is the \texttt{LEAK\_SUSPECTED} rule: once the pump is off, the supply line should carry no flow at all. It runs locally and therefore survives an outage, but it only sees the pipe section carrying the sensor and needs enough flow to turn the turbine, so with flow derived from level it is inactive.

The backend mechanism detects a sustained abnormal consumption level, the small slow shift discussed in Section~\ref{sec:o2-6} \cite{page1954}. The day is divided into 48 slots of 30 minutes, and each slot keeps a baseline mean and variance of the volume used in it, updated by an exponentially weighted moving average:
\begin{equation}
\mu_k \leftarrow \mu_k + \alpha (V_k - \mu_k), \qquad
\sigma^2_k \leftarrow (1-\alpha)\left[\sigma^2_k + \alpha (V_k - \mu_k)^2\right]
\label{eq:ewma}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha = 0.2$. Once a slot has been seen on at least three days, an alert \texttt{LEAK\_BASELINE} is raised when $V_k > \mu_k + 3\sigma_k$ holds for two consecutive slots. Slicing the day reflects that domestic consumption varies strongly with the hour, and the two slot requirement is an accumulation step in the spirit of the cumulative sum method \cite{page1954}, suppressing alarms from a single unusual draw.

Two safeguards were added after the detector learned from bad data. A slot in which the counter went backwards, because it was reset, or in which more water was recorded than the pump can deliver in 30 minutes, is skipped entirely rather than learned, since early noisy flow readings had accumulated thousands of phantom litres. And the baseline is rebuilt from the stored history at every backend start. The honest status is that after four bench days no slot has reached the three day minimum, so the detector is built and tested but has not yet raised a genuine alert.

\section{Pump health classification}
\label{sec:o6-7}

The second advanced option in the assignment is to use the consistency between pump current and flow to classify pump faults. The firmware assigns the pump one of six labels on every cycle:

\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{idle}: the pump is off.
\item \texttt{starting}: on for less than 2 seconds, or less than 25 seconds with no evidence yet.
\item \texttt{ok}: current is present and the water is moving, meaning the level rate $\hat{v}$ exceeds 0.010 centimetres per second (or the turbine reads flow, when that source is selected).
\item \texttt{no\_current}: commanded on but no current, pointing at the electrical side: an open wire, a failed relay, a dead motor.
\item \texttt{dry}: current present but below the 270 milliampere level of a pump moving water, which is the signature of a motor spinning in air.
\item \texttt{no\_flow}: normal current but no water moving after 25 seconds, pointing at the hydraulic side downstream of the pump: a blocked or detached pipe, or a drain that matches the pump.
\end{itemize}

The label is diagnostic, not protective: stopping the pump remains the job of \texttt{NO\_CURRENT} and \texttt{NO\_PROGRESS}. Its value is that the same symptom, a tank that does not fill, is split into two causes that send the operator to two different places, and that the label is captured at the instant of every fault, before the pump is stopped, and stored with the fault record. The 25 second evidence window is chosen from the measured fill rate: at 0.036 centimetres per second of net rise, 25 seconds produce 0.9 centimetres, well clear of the filter noise.
