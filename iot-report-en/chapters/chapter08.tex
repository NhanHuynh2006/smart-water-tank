\chapter{EXPERIMENTS, RESULTS AND CONCLUSION}

\section{Principles and reference instruments}

Three principles apply to the whole experimental programme: every measurement must have a reference independent of the system under evaluation; every experiment must be repeated enough times to yield a dispersion figure and not merely a mean; and results must be reported through standard statistical indicators rather than qualitative remarks \cite{bentley2005, jcgm100}. The references are a millimetre scale standing in the tank, the geometry of the tank itself (a 10 by 10 centimetre base, so one centimetre of level is 0.1 litres), a measuring jug, and the server clock. The indicators are the per point error $e_i = x_{meas,i} - x_{ref,i}$, the mean absolute error and the root mean square error.

All figures in this chapter come from the database of the running system or from two scripts in the repository that read it: \texttt{tools/analyze\_experiments.py} for the automatic cycles, switching and latency, and \texttt{tools/bench.py} for the interactive tests (level calibration, fault injection with a timestamped mark, and network outage). The analysed session is 23 September, 16:48 to 18:12, 4870 telemetry messages on the final filter configuration, with 99.5 percent of messages carrying a trusted level and no fault raised in the window. The test procedures are written out step by step in \texttt{docs/TESTING.md} so that any member can repeat them.

The pull ups removed mains pickup completely. The pump interference, forty times the real signal, was unaffected by the diode and by moving the wiring apart, which places it in the shared supply and ground rather than in radiation. The outlet draw of 0.11 to 0.20 litres per minute is in any case below the 0.3 litre per minute starting flow of the sensor. Flow for control and accounting is therefore derived from the level (Section 6.2), and the result is judged by the volume error below.

\textbf{E2, volume estimation error.} For each automatic fill the device's pumped volume is compared with a reference built only from the level and the tank geometry:
\begin{equation}
V_{ref} = A\,\Delta h + \bar{Q}_{drain}\,T_{fill},
\end{equation}
where $\Delta h$ is the level rise over the fill, $T_{fill}$ its duration, and $\bar{Q}_{drain}$ the drain rate measured independently by fitting the level slope with the pump off for 90 seconds before the fill and 90 seconds after it (skipping 20 seconds after the stop while the pipe empties). Averaging before and after matters because the drain follows Torricelli's law and is faster when the tank is fuller. The device value is the constant delivery integrated over the run, so the comparison tests the calibrated constant and the timing, not a sensor.

The mean volume error is $-5.2$ percent with a spread from $-15.2$ to $+8.2$ percent. \section{Control response and switching}

\textbf{E3, control response.} Figure \ref{fig:cycles} shows the analysed session. Every automatic fill stopped between 70.1 and 70.8 percent against a 70 percent threshold, and the level peaked 1.1 to 1.85 percent higher, the water still in the pipe after the relay opens. The worst peak, 72.4 percent, leaves 12.6 percent of margin to the 85 percent overflow limit. A fill from the lower threshold took 144 to 160 seconds at a net rise of 0.033 to 0.037 centimetres per second.

One result was not predicted. The automatic fills did not start at 30 percent but at 32 to 33 percent, and in every case the lower float engaged in the same second. The float is mounted slightly higher than the 30 percent line, about 4.5 centimetres, and because the controller accepts either the float or the ultrasonic threshold as permission to start, the float wins. This is the designed behaviour of two independent start paths, and the finding is that the effective lower threshold is set by a screw on the tank wall rather than by the configuration.

\textbf{E4, switching frequency.} Over the 84 minute session the pump started 18 times, 12.9 per hour, including starts commanded by hand during the command tests. The shortest gap between a stop and the next start was 32 seconds, above the 20 second minimum off time, so the relay never chattered. The level was trusted in 99.5 percent of messages, and no fault fired, which bounds the false alarm rate of all eleven rules at zero in 84 minutes of normal operation.

\textbf{A fault found by this analysis.} Separating automatic from manual starts exposed pump starts that belonged to neither: starts at 38, 46 and 51 percent with the device in automatic mode and no command sent. Each coincided with a device restart, identified by the sequence number returning to one, and in the replayed history the pump turned on in the second message after boot with the level reading exactly zero. The cause was an initialisation order defect. At start up the firmware sets the time of the last valid reading to the current time, so that the sensor timeout counts from power up; but the validity flag was derived from that same timestamp, so for the first seconds after every boot the level was declared valid while still holding its initial value of zero. The state machine saw an empty tank and started the pump, which then ran until the real level passed 70 percent. The fix requires at least one accepted reading before the level can be declared valid, and the status message now carries the reason for the last restart, so a restart caused by a supply dip can be told apart from a firmware upload.

\textbf{E8, control policy comparison.} To show that the band and timing constraints matter, configuration A (49 and 51 percent, no timing constraints, approximating a single threshold) was compared with configuration B (30 and 70 percent with both timing constraints) on the simulator of Section 5.4, under the same ten minute continuous draw repeated three times, with one fixed target band from 25 to 85 percent for both. A switched 472 times per hour and B 8 times per hour, a reduction of 98.3 percent, with neither leaving the target band. The hardware session above, at 12.9 starts per hour with manual starts included and a different draw rate, is of the same order as the simulated figure for B.

\section{Latency and behaviour during an outage}

\textbf{E7, and why the headline number is the round trip.} The group reports the command round trip as the primary latency result, not the one way telemetry delay. A one way delay is $t_{recv} - t_{send}$ read from two different clocks, so it carries their offset as a systematic error, and the network time protocol on a microcontroller over Wi-Fi is accurate only to tens of milliseconds, the same order as the quantity measured. Immediately after a reboot the group observed an apparent one way delay of about 1100 milliseconds while the round trip at the same moment was 187 milliseconds, direct evidence that the one way figure was reporting clock skew. The round trip is stamped by the server at both ends, so the offset cancels exactly.

\textbf{A measurement defect found and corrected.} The first implementation carried a device timestamp at one second resolution, whose quantisation error of up to 500 milliseconds exceeded the delay being measured: the median of 844.6 milliseconds over 372 samples was an artefact of the field width. A millisecond field was added alongside, and the same measurement then gave 245.0 milliseconds. The resolution of an instrument must be finer than the effect it is meant to resolve, otherwise the instrument measures itself.

\textbf{E6, the outage procedure.} The outage test isolates the device alone, leaving the broker and the backend connected to each other, because that is the failure the requirement describes. \texttt{bench.py outage} either drops the device's traffic at the laptop firewall for a fixed interval or prompts the operator to switch the access point off and on, records every MQTT message, and reports the time until the broker published the last will, the time to reconnect, the messages replayed and lost, every pump transition contained in the replayed history, and the longest control period during the outage. An earlier attempt stopped the broker process instead; that is not equivalent, because the device reconnected one second before the backend resubscribed and the whole replayed batch was published to a broker with no subscriber, so none of it reached storage.

\textbf{Reading the outage result.} Thirty two entries against a buffer of 240 means the buffer was about thirteen percent full, so a four minute outage remains within capacity. The ten lost messages deserve more attention than the thirty two that survived. They were published into a socket that the client still believed open: a connection severed by a silent packet drop is detected only when the 15 second keep alive expires, and every sample generated inside that window is written to a dead socket. The residual gap is bounded by the keep alive interval, not by the buffer size. The E6 figures above were recorded on the first firmware, in which a blocked broker could stall the control loop for three seconds per reconnection attempt; the final firmware bounds that stall to half a second and reports it, and the test is to be repeated on it with the pump running at the moment of the cut, so that the replayed history shows the device stopping the pump at 70 percent on its own.

\textbf{E9.} The spread of the first round trip figures was wider than a local wireless network should produce, with a 95th percentile near 900 milliseconds at a signal strength better than $-20$ dBm. \section{Limitations}

The group believes that pointing precisely at where the design falls short carries as much engineering value as presenting what worked.

On hardware, the most serious limitation is the flow measurement. The assignment makes a flow sensor mandatory, and two are fitted, but in this installation they do not measure water: the pump's conducted interference is forty times the real pulse rate, and the outlet draw is below the sensor's starting flow. Flow is therefore derived from the level, which makes the inlet a calibrated constant rather than an independent observation. Three consequences follow and are stated rather than hidden: the volume error of $-5$ percent with a 23 point spread is a property of that constant; the literal rule ``pump on with near zero flow'' is carried by level progress instead of the turbine; and the edge leak rule is inactive. The remedies are known and cheap: power the sensors from a supply that does not share a return path with the motor, filter the interrupt input by minimum pulse width (a real pulse at 0.36 litres per minute lasts about 14 milliseconds, the interference well under one), and choose a sensor whose range starts near 0.05 litres per minute.

Second, the current scale factor assumes the 5 ampere sensor variant and a one to one divider; it yields 360 to 425 milliamperes for a pump rated at 100 to 200, so either the divider or the variant differs from the assumption, and one reading with a multimeter in series will settle it. The binary use of current, present or absent, which is all the fault rules and the health classifier need, is unaffected. Third, the beam of the ultrasonic sensor is wider than the tank, so a share of pings return from the wall or the floor; the filter chain of Section 6.2 makes this manageable, and the counters show 93 percent acceptance, but a stilling tube or a wider vessel would remove the cause. Fourth, the sensor can lock up after a supply disturbance and recover only when its supply is cycled; powering it from a controllable output would let the firmware do that by itself.

On measurement, the headline latency no longer relies on two clocks, as Section 8.5 explains; the residual limitation is that the clock synchronisation error was characterised only by observation. The outage buffer protects only samples generated after the client notices the broken connection, so up to one keep alive interval, 15 seconds, is lost at the start of every outage; and an outage that also restarts the broker discards the replayed batch, since the device can flush before the backend resubscribes. Repeat counts of six to seventy five suffice for means and spreads but not for formal hypothesis tests. The rolling baseline needs at least three observations of each half hour slot before it may alarm; after three bench days none of the 48 slots qualifies, so its detection performance is unmeasured. The level calibration and three of the fault delays of Table \ref{tab:e5} remain to be run on the final installation.

On operations, the public tunnel address changes every time it is started, which suits a demonstration but not a deployment, where a named tunnel or a fixed host is needed; administrator sessions are held in memory and do not survive a restart; and there is one administrator password rather than per user accounts. The broker traffic is not encrypted inside the laboratory network. There is no remote firmware update.

On the interface, a defect that survived three separate inspections is worth recording because of how it hid rather than because of its size. The query that feeds the history charts applied its row limit after ordering the window in ascending time, so it returned the oldest rows of the window rather than the newest. At the specified rate of one message per second a ten minute window holds six hundred rows and never reaches the two thousand row limit, so the fault was invisible in every ordinary session. Running the simulator at ten times real time raises the rate to roughly five messages per second, the window then holds close to three thousand rows, and the charts silently froze in the past while the live status panel beside them continued to update. Nothing failed and nothing was logged; the two halves of the same screen simply disagreed.

The general lesson is that a pagination limit at the query layer must truncate from the end that is being discarded, not from the end being displayed. Ordering ascending and then limiting is correct only while the result set is known to fit, which makes it a latent fault that waits for the data rate to rise. The corrected query selects the newest rows first and reorders them for plotting. The group notes that the defect was found only because the acceleration factor of the simulator pushed the system past a threshold the specified rate never approaches, which is an argument for exercising a system deliberately outside its nominal operating point rather than only at it.

Future directions include a working flow measurement by the remedies above, which would restore the inlet as an independent observation and let the turbine and level estimates be checked against each other as a leak test in their own right; a Kalman filter in place of the alpha-beta filter, using the pump state as a known input; continuous speed control by pulse width modulation; and multiple nodes feeding one backend.

\section{Faults found on the bench}

The faults recorded here were all found after the prototype was assembled and nominally working. The group presents them because each was invisible from the code alone and each was resolved by a measurement rather than by argument.

\subsection{An inverted relay made the firmware believe the pump was off}

The most serious fault was also the simplest. The configuration declared the relay module active low, so the firmware drove the control pin high to stop the pump. The module fitted was active high. Every command to stop therefore started the pump, and the telemetry reported \texttt{pump: false} throughout. The tank filled from 0.2 to 11.2 centimetres and came close to overflowing while the interface displayed a stopped pump and an overflow lock.

The fault also corrupted the current measurement, in a way that concealed it. The quiescent offset of the Hall sensor is tracked by an exponential average that updates only while the firmware believes the pump is off. Because the firmware believed that permanently, the average absorbed the running current of the pump as its own zero, and the reported current settled near zero while the motor ran.

Determining the true polarity required care, because the obvious method gives the wrong answer. The Hall current sensor is bidirectional: its output rests at mid scale and moves up or down depending on the direction of current through the primary conductor. A larger reading therefore does not imply a larger current, and the first version of the group's test concluded the opposite of the truth on exactly that basis. The quantity that does separate the two states is the \emph{ripple}, because a brushed motor draws a visibly rough current through its commutator while an open relay draws a flat one. Measured over two rounds, the control pin low gave a mean of 1545.88 millivolts with 4.47 millivolts of ripple, and the pin high gave 1516.54 millivolts with 27.05 millivolts of ripple. The ripple ratio of 6.0 identifies the energised state unambiguously and does not depend on which way the load is wired, whereas the means differ by only 29 millivolts and in the misleading direction.

\subsection{An automatic direction level shifter consumed a third of the echoes}

The echo line of the ultrasonic sensor was initially routed through a TXS0108E translator. That part is intended for bidirectional open drain buses: it carries integrated pull up resistors of about 10 kilohms on both ports and a one shot circuit that accelerates edges. The echo output of an HC-SR04 is a strong push pull driver, and a pulse width is the only carrier of information in the signal, so both mechanisms work against it. Replacing the translator with a two resistor divider on the echo line alone changed the miss rate from between 28 and 30 percent of pings to zero in 179 consecutive samples.

\subsection{Fifty hertz on a floating signal line reads as plausible flow}

Both flow inputs reported between 0.26 and 0.51 litres per minute with the pump stopped. Converting the reading back to its underlying pulse rate showed exactly 50.00 hertz on one channel, unchanging across every sample. The control experiment was to close the drain valve, after which no flow of any kind was physically possible; the channel continued to report 50.00 hertz. A signal locked to the mains frequency and indifferent to whether water can flow is induced interference, not measurement.

The cause is impedance. The internal pull up of the microcontroller is about 45 kilohms, which leaves the line high enough in impedance to act as an antenna. The specified remedy, an external 4.7 kilohm pull up to the 3.3 volt rail, lowers the source impedance by an order of magnitude. A second and larger disturbance appears only while the pump runs, when both channels rise together to about 4750 hertz, a figure that would correspond to 48 litres per minute against a measured pump delivery of 0.36. Two independent sensors agreeing to within 0.7 percent are not measuring water; they are sharing a common mode disturbance injected by the motor.

\subsection{A silently truncated message stopped the database without stopping anything else}

Adding two validity fields to the telemetry payload pushed its length past the 448 byte buffer that served it. The JSON library truncates without raising an error and still returns a string, so the device published well formed nonsense. On the server the parse exception was raised inside a callback whose exceptions the client library discards. The result was a system in which the device published once per second, the broker delivered every message, the health endpoint reported the service as connected, and the database received nothing at all for several minutes while the dashboard displayed a frozen value.

The lesson the group takes is that a serialiser which truncates silently and a callback which swallows exceptions are individually defensible and jointly produce a failure with no symptom at the point of failure. Both ends now report the condition explicitly, and the buffer carries margin above the maximum payload rather than matching it.

\subsection{A command that worked but was never confirmed}

Manual pump commands were executed by the device, yet the command log kept them as pending indefinitely, while mode commands were confirmed normally. The broker log showed the difference: acknowledgements of mode commands were 118 bytes long, acknowledgements of pump commands exactly 256 bytes, the size of the acknowledgement buffer.

The cause was an interaction between two libraries. The MQTT client delivers an incoming message as a pointer into its internal buffer, and the same buffer is reused for outgoing messages. The JSON parser, handed a non-constant pointer, parses in place and returns string references into that buffer rather than copies. A pump command starts the pump, which publishes the new pump state before the acknowledgement is sent; that publish overwrote the buffer, so the command identifier being echoed back had already become arbitrary bytes. Mode commands publish nothing in between and were unaffected. The fix copies the incoming message into a buffer owned by the handler before parsing. The general point is that a zero-copy parser makes the lifetime of the input part of the program's correctness, and that lifetime is decided by another library.

\subsection{A robust filter gated by a fragile statistic}

After the level filter was widened to a fifteen sample median, the group counted, for every ping, which validation stage rejected it. In one ordinary fill of 605 pings, 244 were rejected, and 98 percent of those rejections came from a single stage: the check that the spread of the window stayed below a limit. Missing echoes accounted for only 2 percent. The sensor was answering almost every time.

The spread had been measured as maximum minus minimum. That statistic is controlled entirely by the single most extreme sample, so one stray echo from the tank floor among fifteen good ones was enough to discard the whole window. The median was chosen precisely because it tolerates isolated outliers; gating it with the statistic most sensitive to isolated outliers undid that choice. Replacing the range with the interquartile range, which ignores the top and bottom quarter of the window, raised the acceptance rate from 60 to 93 percent in otherwise identical conditions. The interquartile range still grows when the sensor genuinely alternates between the water surface and the floor, because then roughly half the samples sit in each group, which is exactly the case the check exists to reject.

\subsection{The level sensor is excellent, and unusable, in the same installation}

With the water still, twenty consecutive samples read between 3.87 and 3.89 centimetres, a standard deviation of 0.010 centimetres. With the pump running, the same sensor collapses to reporting an empty tank while the tank is demonstrably filling. The group verified that the validity gates were not the cause by disabling them: the raw reading itself falls to the empty value, so the gates were reporting the data correctly rather than rejecting it wrongly.

This pair of results is the clearest argument in the project for layered protection. Throughout the interval in which the level reading was false and the validity flag had been forced true, the rule that stops the pump when the level fails to rise still fired and still stopped it. That rule consults the clock and the level history, never the validity flag, which is precisely why it survived.

\section{Conclusion}

The project delivers a working closed loop water tank that measures its level, controls its pump, detects and classifies faults, keeps working without the network, and can be watched from anywhere on the Internet while only an administrator can operate it. Every line of firmware, backend and interface was written by the group, and every figure in this chapter can be regenerated from the database by the scripts in the repository.

Three decisions carry the design. First, the whole control law and every safety rule live in the edge controller, derived from the rule that anything needing an immediate safe stop belongs at the edge; the outage requirement then follows from the architecture instead of being added to it. Second, safety is structural: every path that can start the pump passes through one guard function that is re-evaluated every cycle, and overflow is guarded by independent layers of which the raw distance guard, the float and the run limits do not depend on the filtered level at all. Third, the group trusted measurements over datasheets and over its own expectations: the pump delivers a fifth of its rated flow, the flow sensors count interference rather than water, the level sensor needs a fifteen sample robust filter in this tank, and the analysis of the final session found an initialisation defect that started the pump after every restart. Each of these was found by counting, and each is fixed or stated.

On the bench the controller stopped every automatic fill between 70.1 and 70.8 percent with at most 1.85 percent of overshoot, switched 12.9 times per hour with no chatter, raised no false alarm in 84 minutes, estimated pumped volume to $-5$ percent on average, and answered commands with a median round trip of 42 milliseconds. The main open item is a trustworthy flow measurement, for which the cause and the remedy are both identified.
