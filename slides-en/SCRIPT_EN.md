## 1. Smart Water Tank  ·  0:45

Good morning everyone. We are group [number], and our project is Project 08, the Smart Water Tank. In one sentence: we built a small real water tank whose pump is controlled by an ESP32, which measures level and consumption, detects faults, and can be supervised from anywhere through MQTT and a web dashboard. If you remember one idea from this talk, it is this: every control decision and every safety decision is made on the ESP32 itself, so the tank keeps working even when the network does not. The talk has seven parts and takes about fifty minutes, followed by a live demonstration on the real tank.

## 3. 01 · Problem and requirements  ·  0:10

Part one: the problem, and the requirements of the assignment.

## 4. What a smart tank must do  ·  1:05

Most houses in Vietnam still rely on a mechanical float valve in the storage tank. It is cheap and reliable, but it answers exactly one question: is the tank full? The owner cannot see how much water was used, cannot notice a slow leak until the bill arrives, and cannot know that the pump is running dry until it burns out. The assignment asks for six things, shown here. We must measure level and flow against a real reference. We must control the level with a band so the pump does not switch on and off constantly. We must protect the tank so it never overflows, even with a broken sensor or a careless manual command. We must diagnose faults, for example a pump that runs but moves no water. We must supervise everything remotely. And the dark card is the overriding requirement: the tank must keep controlling itself when the network is gone. That last requirement shaped our whole architecture.

## 5. Requirements and where they are met  ·  1:10

This table takes each requirement of the brief, says how we meet it, and gives an honest status; it is the same as Table 1.3.1 in the report. Most rows are met and demonstrated. Two points I want to be upfront about. First, the flow sensors: both are fitted, wired and read, and since the rewiring of the fifth of October the inlet sensor counts real water and is calibrated against the ruler. But control and the volume counter still use flow derived from the level, and the outlet sensor is below its starting flow at our drain rates, so we call this requirement partly validated, not simply met. Second, the advanced component: we built both options the brief offers, a rolling baseline for abnormal consumption and a classifier based on pump current versus flow. The classifier works and we will demo it; the baseline needs three days of data per half hour slot before it may alarm, and our bench days do not reach that yet.

## 6. 02 · Architecture and hardware  ·  0:10

Part two: the architecture and the hardware.

## 7. System architecture  ·  1:20

This is the whole system on one slide, and it is the same diagram as Figure 2.1.1 in the report. Inside the dashed blue boundary is the edge node: the sensors feed the ESP32 firmware, which runs a closed control loop every 200 milliseconds and switches the relay and the pump directly. That loop keeps working when everything below the boundary is gone. The ESP32 publishes telemetry, state, faults and its last will to a Mosquitto broker on the laptop, and receives commands on the cmd topic, each answered on cmd/ack. A FastAPI backend subscribes, stores everything in six SQLite tables, and serves the web dashboard through a REST API; commands from the dashboard go back as POST requests. Finally a Cloudflare tunnel publishes the dashboard on a public HTTPS address, so anyone can watch from anywhere without opening an inbound port. Mapped onto the five layer model: perception is the ESP32 and its sensors, transport is MQTT over Wi-Fi, processing is FastAPI and SQLite, application is the dashboard. Notice the dark box: the only path to the pump runs through it. The server can ask; it never switches.

## 8. The server never switches the pump. It asks, and the ESP32 decides.  ·  1:00

This is the single most important decision in the project. The lectures give a placement rule: any operation that needs an immediate safe stop must be computed at the edge. We applied it strictly. Everything that needs a deterministic cycle or has to survive an outage lives on the ESP32: filtering, the state machine, all eleven fault rules, the safety guard, the pump health classifier and the volume accumulation. The server keeps only what the microcontroller cannot do well: long history, the daily report, and a leak baseline that needs days of memory. The consequence is graceful degradation. When the network drops, the user loses visibility and remote commands, but the tank still refills at the correct thresholds and every safety rule still applies. The outage requirement becomes a property of the architecture, not a feature we bolted on.

## 9. The bench rig  ·  1:20

Our tank is deliberately small: a ten by ten centimetre base, so one centimetre of level is exactly a tenth of a litre. The sensor face is 15.88 centimetres above the floor, six and a quarter inches on the ruler, and the working range is the lowest 12.4 centimetres, 1.24 litres, which leaves 3.5 centimetres of air under the sensor at a full tank. A full fill and drain cycle takes a few minutes, so we can observe many control cycles in one session. The orange number is the most important measurement on this slide. The pump datasheet promises 1.67 litres per minute. We measured the real delivery by closing the outlet and timing the level: 1.2 centimetres in 20 seconds, which is 0.36 litres per minute, almost five times less, because of the delivery head and the tubing. And it is not even constant: on the fifth of October, with a lower source bucket, the same pump gave 0.24. Every timing threshold in our firmware is sized from measured numbers, not from the datasheet. The table lists the sensors and how each signal is conditioned for the 3.3 volt inputs of the ESP32.

## 11. Signal wiring and power path  ·  1:35

This slide matches Figures 2.4.1 and 2.4.2 of the report. On the left, every pin of the ESP32 and how its signal is conditioned. The ultrasonic echo is a five volt pulse, so it goes through a 10 and 20 kilo-ohm divider; the trigger can be driven directly. Both flow sensors are open collector outputs with 4.7 kilo-ohm pull ups to 3.3 volts, which removed the fifty hertz pickup we had with the weak internal pull ups. The current sensor output passes a one to one divider into GPIO 34, an input only pin. The relay moved to GPIO 10 on the fifth of October. That pin is normally wired to the flash chip, and it is free only when the flash runs in dual line DIO mode, so the build sets that mode; we checked on the bench that the relay then switches without resetting the chip. On the right, the power path. Twelve volts through a two ampere fuse into a five volt, five ampere buck converter. Every load leaves the converter terminal on its own wire, in a star. The pump path goes through the relay contact first, then the current sensor, then the pump, so the measured current is exactly the pump current. The pump carries a flyback diode and a 100 nanofarad capacitor at its terminals, and a 470 microfarad capacitor on the controller board buffers the inrush.

## 12. Three signal lessons, each found by measuring  ·  1:55

Three signal lessons, each found by measuring rather than by reading code. First, the ultrasonic echo line originally went through a TXS0108E automatic level shifter. That part is designed for open drain buses, and its pull ups and edge accelerators distorted the echo pulse, whose width is the measurement. We lost about thirty percent of the echoes. A plain resistor divider fixed it completely: zero misses in 179 pings. Second, the flow inputs reported flow with the valve closed. The pulse rate was exactly 50.00 hertz, the mains frequency: the weak internal pull up turned the wire into an antenna. External 4.7 kilo-ohm pull ups brought it to zero. Third, with the pump running, both flow lines read about 1500 hertz until the twenty second of September, against a real 35. A diode and moving the wires apart changed nothing, so the noise was conducted through the supply and ground. On the fifth of October we rebuilt the power stage: relay ahead of the current sensor, a diode and capacitor at the motor, a 470 microfarad capacitor on the board, the flow inputs swapped. Since then the inlet reads zero with the pump off and 20 to 30 hertz of real water with it on. We calibrated it against the ruler, 85.5 pulses per litre per minute, and it now feeds the tank model. But we say it precisely: the sensor requirement is implemented and partly validated. Control and the volume counter still use flow derived from the level, because the turbine has been validated on one calibration run, not over complete cycles. The outlet sensor is below its starting flow at our drain rates.

## 13. 03 · MQTT protocol  ·  0:10

Part three: the communication protocol.

## 14. MQTT topic design  ·  1:10

Our topic tree goes from broad to specific: water tank, site, device. We chose the quality of service per topic by asking one question: what does it cost to lose one message? Telemetry is a one hertz stream, so losing one sample changes no conclusion, and it uses QoS zero. The pump state is published only when it changes, so it uses QoS one and is retained, which means a dashboard that opens later immediately receives the current state. Faults, commands and acknowledgements use QoS one because losing any of them is a functional or safety failure. The status topic carries the last will message: if the ESP32 disappears without a clean disconnect, the broker itself publishes "offline". We never use QoS two; duplicate commands are already handled by a unique command identifier, so the device just repeats the same acknowledgement. Every message carries a sequence number and a millisecond timestamp, which is how we count lost messages exactly. And the broker has an access control list: even a compromised ESP32 cannot publish pump commands.

## 15. Commands with acknowledgement  ·  1:30

This diagram follows one press of the Pump ON button. The dashboard posts the command to the backend with the administrator's session cookie. The backend logs it with a fresh command identifier, publishes it on the command topic, and immediately answers "pending". At this point the button has not changed; the screen only says "sending". The ESP32 receives the command and decides: is it in manual mode, does the safety guard allow a start, has the minimum off time elapsed? It then publishes an acknowledgement carrying the outcome, the reason and the real pump state. The dashboard polls the command log and shows the result as a plain sentence, while the pump symbol follows only the telemetry. This is the feedback sync principle from the lectures. Two lessons came from this path. First, a user once reported that manual mode did not work; in fact the device was correctly refusing a restart inside the twenty second rest period, and the interface was silent about it. Now it shows the reason and a countdown. Second, pump commands were executed but never confirmed: the MQTT library reuses one buffer for incoming and outgoing messages, and the JSON parser was reading in place, so publishing the pump state overwrote the command identifier. We now copy the payload before parsing.

## 16. 04 · Control and intelligence  ·  0:10

Part four, the core of the project: control and intelligence on the ESP32.

## 17. Level measurement pipeline  ·  1:50

Now the core. The ultrasonic sensor sits 15.88 centimetres above the tank floor, so the level is 15.88 minus the measured distance. That sounds trivial, but the beam spreads about fifteen degrees and is already wider than our ten centimetre tank when it reaches the water, so some echoes come back from the wall or the floor. We therefore never trust a single ping. Each reading passes five gates. We keep a window of the last fifteen pings, and any distance more than a centimetre beyond the floor is discarded, because only a stray echo can produce it. If the interquartile range of the window is larger than 3.5 centimetres, the sensor is jumping between two surfaces, and we reject the window. Otherwise we take the lower quartile, not the median: a weak echo is detected one or more 40 kilohertz cycles late, about 0.43 centimetres each, so errors only ever lengthen the distance and the shorter readings are the trustworthy ones. Then a physics check rejects changes faster than the pump could cause, or a level below the filtered level while the pump is filling. Finally an alpha-beta filter tracks both the level and its rate, without the lag of a moving average. Every gate has its own rejection counter in the telemetry. That is how we found that ninety-eight percent of rejections came from one gate that used maximum minus minimum; switching to the interquartile range raised acceptance from sixty to ninety-three percent. And separately, for safety, a raw distance check bypasses all of this: three pings closer than 4.5 centimetres trigger the overflow protection, whatever the filter believes.

## 18. Model bridging and flow from level  ·  1:45

We also run a simple model of the tank in parallel with the measurement. The level changes by inflow minus outflow, divided by the base area. Since the fifth of October the inflow is the inlet flow sensor whenever it reads flow, and the calibrated constant otherwise; that matters because the pump delivery changes with the source bucket, from 0.36 to 0.24 litres per minute, and a drifting model cannot judge whether a new reading is believable. The model is pulled gently towards every accepted reading. Its first use is bridging: when the ultrasonic sensor produces no accepted reading, for example in its blind zone around sixty percent, the state machine controls on the model, for at most ninety seconds; after that the pump stops and we raise LEVEL_LOST. A returning reading that disagrees with the model by more than 2.5 centimetres is rejected. Our first firmware simply stopped every such fill at about fifty percent. Its second use is the flow shown on the dashboard. For accounting, inflow is still the calibrated pump delivery while the pump runs, and outflow is the falling rate of the level times the area. One subtlety: the first version computed outflow as pump delivery minus the net rise at every instant; the pump term switches instantly but the rate estimate lags, so every pump start made the outflow curve jump. Physically the valve sets the draw, not the pump, so we measure outflow only when the pump has been off for twenty seconds, and hold it while pumping.

## 19. Controller state machine  ·  1:35

The controller is a state machine with seven states, the same set as Table 3.3.1 in the report. At boot the very first instruction drives the relay off, and the device stays in BOOT until a first level reading has been accepted, so the zero the level variable holds at power up can never be read as an empty tank. Then it waits in IDLE. In automatic mode, when the level falls below thirty percent, or the low float engages, it starts the pump and enters FILLING; above seventy percent, after at least three seconds, it stops and returns to IDLE. The arrow over the top is the manual path: an operator command enters MANUAL_ON, but only if the safety guard allows it. That band from thirty to seventy is our hysteresis, and with a minimum on time of three seconds and a minimum off time of twenty seconds it prevents chattering. Any fault rule, from FILLING or MANUAL_ON, stops the pump and moves to one of three fault states. Why a state machine and not a simple if statement? Because fault states must be sticky. Sensor faults clear themselves after two and a half seconds of good signal, the dashed arrow. A pump fault or an overflow lock waits for an operator, otherwise a pump that sucked air would restart the moment the hose touches water again.

## 20. Eleven fault rules  ·  1:25

The brief asks for at least one fault rule; we implemented eleven, the same list as Table 3.4.1 in the report. They run every two hundred milliseconds, in priority order, and any rule that fires stops the pump at once and publishes a fault message. The first three concern the signal and clear themselves when the signal returns. The others lock the pump until an operator clears them, except the leak warning. Two rows are highlighted. OVERFLOW has three independent triggers: the mechanical float, the raw distance, or the filtered level. NO_PROGRESS is the physical form of the brief's example rule, pump on with near zero flow. Its literal form, DRY_RUN, uses the turbine signal, and because control does not use the turbine, DRY_RUN is disabled when flow is derived from level: a guard that can never fire is worse than none. NO_PROGRESS uses a quantity we do measure: the pump draws current but the level does not rise. The sensor timeouts are long on purpose: crossing the blind zone takes up to seventy five seconds and the model covers that gap, so the limit is ninety seconds while the model is valid and forty five otherwise. Our first version used four seconds and stopped every fill early.

## 21. Safety guard and overflow layers  ·  1:15

The brief says manual mode must not bypass safety. We solved that with structure, not discipline. Every code path that can start the pump, automatic or manual, goes through one function, pumpBlockReason, which returns either nothing or the reason for refusing: an active fault, the upper float, water too close to the sensor, the overflow limit, an untrusted level, or the minimum off time. While a manual pump is running, the same function is re-evaluated every two hundred milliseconds, so lifting the float stops it within one cycle. To verify safety, a reviewer reads one function instead of the whole codebase. On the right are the independent overflow layers. The first two rely on the filtered level and fail together if the sensor fails. The raw distance check bypasses the filter. The progress rule catches a frozen reading. The volume and time caps work even with every level sensor lost, although honestly they only bound the damage. The mechanical float needs no measurement at all, and the relay is driven off as the very first instruction at boot. No single failure disables overflow protection.

## 22. Pump health classification  ·  1:25

The brief offers two options for the advanced component, and we built both. The first is pump health classification, using the consistency between pump current and water flow. When the pump is on and current flows and the water is moving, the label is ok. When current flows but no water moves after twenty-five seconds, the label is no_flow, which points to the hydraulic side: a dry intake, a blocked or detached pipe. When the relay is closed but no current flows, the label is no_current, which points to the electrical side: an open wire, a failed relay or a dead motor. The same symptom, a tank that does not fill, is split into two causes that send the technician to two different places. Evidence of water is the level rising faster than one hundredth of a centimetre per second; the real rise is about 0.036, so twenty-five seconds of pumping gives almost a centimetre, well above the filter noise. The label is diagnostic only; stopping the pump is still the job of the fault rules. But it is captured at the moment of every fault and stored with it. In the demo we will lift the intake out of the water and watch it change to no_flow.

## 23. Rolling baseline and daily volume  ·  1:25

The second advanced option is anomaly detection with a rolling baseline, which runs on the backend because it needs days of memory. Domestic water use depends strongly on the hour, so a single threshold would be too sensitive at night and too lax in the evening. We cut the day into forty-eight half-hour slots. Each slot learns its own normal consumption with an exponentially weighted mean and variance. An alarm is raised when consumption exceeds the mean plus three standard deviations in two consecutive slots, which suppresses one-off events like washing a motorbike. We learned two safeguards the hard way: early noisy flow readings had accumulated thousands of phantom litres, so slots with a counter reset or a physically impossible volume are skipped, and the baseline is rebuilt from the database whenever the backend restarts. Honest status: a slot needs three days of data before it may alarm, and after four bench days, held at different hours, none qualifies yet, so we can show the mechanism but not a real detection. On the right is the daily volume report from the API, pumped in versus drawn out. The query sums the increments between consecutive rows, so a counter reset adds nothing instead of a huge negative step.

## 24. 05 · Backend, dashboard, security  ·  0:10

Part five: the platform around the device, meaning storage, the dashboard, security, and outage behaviour.

## 25. Backend and data model  ·  1:05

The backend is one Python process. A paho MQTT client subscribes to the six topics and writes each one to its own table: telemetry, pump events, faults, availability and commands, plus a configuration table. We chose SQLite on purpose. The lectures explain why relational engines struggle with high-rate time series, but that happens at thousands of writes per second; we write one row per second from one node, so a dedicated time series engine would add operational work for no measurable gain. We still follow the schema principle from the lectures: only the timestamp is indexed, and the sequence number, which changes on every row, is deliberately not, to avoid an index explosion. The REST API is on the right. Reads use GET because they are safe. The configuration uses PUT because replacing the threshold set is idempotent. Commands use POST because each one creates a new record. The two highlighted endpoints change the physical world, so they require an administrator session.

## 26. Dashboard and remote access  ·  1:20

This is the dashboard. It is a single HTML page with no framework. The top part is a process mimic in the style of a control room: the source bucket, the pump, the pipe and the tank, with the water drawn at its measured height against a ruler, and the thirty, seventy and overflow lines at their true positions, so anyone can see why the pump just switched. Below are the tiles for level, flow, volume and pump current with the health label, and next to them the level history. A freshness indicator shows how many seconds old the data is. For this screenshot, we replayed recorded bench data into an isolated copy of the system, so the real database was not touched. The dashboard is public: a Cloudflare tunnel gives it an HTTPS address, so it can be watched from any phone, without opening a port on our network. Control is private: the buttons stay hidden until the browser has an administrator session. We first tried to allow control only from the host machine's address, and rejected it: behind the tunnel every request arrives from 127.0.0.1, so that rule would have given control to the whole Internet.

## 27. Security model  ·  1:40

The ITU model in the lectures treats security as a capability that cuts across every layer, so we analysed threats layer by layer. At the broker, anonymous access is disabled, and an access control list follows least privilege: the device account cannot publish commands, so even a compromised ESP32 cannot drive the pump. At the API, every endpoint that changes the physical world requires a session. The password lives in a file outside the repository and is compared in constant time; a correct password produces a random 256-bit token in an HttpOnly, SameSite Strict, Secure cookie; and five wrong attempts in five minutes lock that address. The orange row is the gap we state plainly: the MQTT path inside the lab is not encrypted. The broker listens on port 1883 inside the laboratory network only, and the public tunnel carries the dashboard over HTTPS and never exposes the broker. But someone capturing Wi-Fi traffic in the lab could read telemetry and the broker passwords. So we do not claim end-to-end TLS; MQTTS on port 8883 is the deployment step, at the cost of a public key handshake on the ESP32. The two blue rows are the interesting ones, because the control design itself stops them: forged telemetry cannot cause an overflow, because the float and the raw distance guard are on the device, and command spam cannot wear out the pump, because the minimum on and off times are enforced in firmware.

## 28. Behaviour during a network outage  ·  1:35

What happens when the network fails? Four mechanisms. First, the control loop never waits for the network. The lectures warn against connection logic inside a blocking loop. Opening a TCP socket to an unreachable broker still blocks inside the network stack, three seconds by default, so we cap it at half a second. Our outage test found a second trap: writing to a socket whose peer has silently vanished blocked the loop for up to ten seconds. Now the firmware checks, without waiting, that the socket can take data before every publish, and closes it after three seconds of refusals. The device publishes its longest loop period in the first message after reconnecting, so the claim can be checked: 0.88 seconds in our final test. Second, nothing is forgotten: while offline, telemetry goes into a ring buffer of 240 samples, four minutes at one hertz, replayed in order when the link returns. Volume is saved to flash every minute. Third, everyone knows: the broker publishes the last will after one and a half keep alive intervals, 21.8 seconds in the test, and the dashboard turns red after five seconds without data. Fourth, the device finds its way back: exponential backoff, a Wi-Fi join attempted at most every ten seconds, because restarting it every second never let it finish, up to three networks, and the broker found again by its mDNS name.

## 29. 06 · Experiments and results  ·  0:10

Part six: experiments and results.

## 30. Experiment plan  ·  1:05

Our experimental rule was simple: every measurement needs a reference that is independent of the system being measured, and every number must come from the database or a log by a script that anyone can rerun. The scripts are in the repository: analyze_experiments for cycles, switching and latency; bench for outage tests; the stepwise calibration program; and systest for the automated acceptance test. Two sessions are analysed. The control session is the twenty third of September, an hour and twenty-four minutes: 4870 messages, 99.5 percent with a trusted level, and not a single fault raised. The calibration and acceptance session is the fifth of October, on the final firmware and the final sensor geometry: the ruler calibration, the outage test with the pump running, and the system test. One honest note on E5: the no current rule has not been triggered by a physical injection; we show what we did measure on the E5 slide.

## 31. Level calibration against a ruler  ·  1:30

Experiment one, the level calibration, run on the fifth of October on the final geometry. With the drain valve closed, the pump runs in twenty second steps; after each step it stops, the water settles for twelve seconds, and we record forty raw pings. The inlet flow sensor counts every litre pumped, so the reference level after each step is one ruler reading, 4.9 centimetres, plus the pumped volume divided by the hundred square centimetre base. To be precise about what this proves: the turbine factor was set from the same first pass, so the low points test linearity; the second pass reused that factor unchanged. Outside one band the sensor is good: ten points with a root mean square error of 0.68 centimetres and a mean absolute error of 0.53. Inside the band, with the water surface 7.9 to 8.7 centimetres below the transducer, every one of forty pings returns the same wrong value, a stray echo: 1.0 centimetre for a true 7.2, and a distance beyond the floor for a true 8.0. Because it is stable it cannot be filtered as noise. This explained why fills used to stall around sixty percent. The geometry gate now discards anything beyond the floor, the model gate rejects the rest, and the tank model bridges the band, up to ninety seconds.

## 32. Control response and switching  ·  1:30

This is the level over seventy-six minutes on the bench. Blue bands are the pump running; hatched areas are manual mode, which we used for command tests and to drain the tank below the band on purpose. In automatic mode, every fill stopped between 70.1 and 70.8 percent against a seventy percent threshold. The level then rises a little more, 1.4 percent on average and 1.85 at most, because water still in the pipe keeps arriving after the relay opens. The highest peak, 72.4 percent, leaves more than twelve points of margin to the overflow line in orange. A fill from the bottom of the band takes about two and a half minutes. Over the session the pump started 12.9 times per hour, including manual starts, and the shortest rest between a stop and the next start was 32 seconds, above our twenty second minimum, so the relay never chattered. And not one fault rule fired falsely. One surprise: automatic fills started at 32 to 33 percent, not 30. In every case the low float engaged in that same second. The float is mounted slightly above the thirty percent line, and because either path may start the pump, the float wins. The effective threshold is set by a screw on the tank wall.

## 33. Volume estimation error  ·  1:25

How accurate is the accumulated volume? For each automatic fill we compare the device's pumped volume with a reference built only from the level and the tank geometry: the rise in level times the base area, plus what drained out during the fill. The drain rate is measured independently, from the slope of the level with the pump off, ninety seconds before and after the fill, and averaged, because by Torricelli's law the drain is faster when the tank is fuller. Over six complete fills on the twenty third of September, the mean error is minus 5.2 percent, ranging from minus 15 to plus 8. The pattern is informative: the three largest negative errors coincide with the three largest drain rates. That is what a constant pump delivery predicts, since the real pump output varies with the water level in the source bucket while our model keeps 0.36. The fifth of October confirmed it: with a lower bucket the same pump delivered 0.24 litres per minute, a third less. So this error belongs to treating delivery as a constant. The inlet sensor now works and is calibrated; moving the volume counter to it, after validating it over complete cycles, is our next step.

## 34. Command latency  ·  1:30

For latency, our headline number is the command round trip, not the one way telemetry delay, and the reason is methodological. A one way delay subtracts two clocks, and network time on a microcontroller is only accurate to tens of milliseconds, the same order as what we measure; once, right after a reboot, the one way figure read 1100 milliseconds while the round trip at that moment was 187. The round trip is stamped by the server at both ends, so the skew cancels. The chart shows how it improved. The first firmware had a median of 226 milliseconds. The big step was the radio: the ESP32 Arduino core enables modem sleep by default, so the receiver sleeps between beacons and packets wait; turning it off cut the 95th percentile from 884 to 315, at the cost of roughly double radio current, fine for a mains powered device. On the twenty third of September we measured a median of 42 and a 95th percentile of 138 over 75 commands. On the final firmware, in the system test of the fifth of October, forty commands gave a median of 29 milliseconds and a 95th percentile of 48.5, with none lost. Those sessions also used different access points, so we do not attribute the last steps to a single cause.

## 35. Fault detection  ·  1:45

Fault detection. These rows come from the fault, pump event and command logs of the twenty first to twenty third of September. Unplugging the echo wire raised SENSOR_TIMEOUT forty to forty one seconds after the last trusted message, which matches the forty five second design of that firmware once you account for the five seconds before a reading is marked untrusted. The final firmware lengthens this to ninety seconds while the tank model is valid, because crossing the blind zone takes up to seventy five seconds; that value has not been re-timed. In all eighteen overflow and no progress events where the pump was running, the pump off message went out in the same two hundred millisecond cycle as the fault. Every manual start during a fault was refused with the right reason. We are upfront about the orange row: we never pulled a pump wire to trigger NO_CURRENT, so its two seconds is a design value, not a measurement. What we did measure is the signal it depends on: with the relay closed the current read 471 to 487 milliamps in every message, and zero after it opened, a margin of about eleven times the noise floor. And NO_PROGRESS fired twice for real on the fifth of October: the drain valve let out as much as the pump delivered at forty eight percent, the level hovered for ninety seconds, and the rule stopped the pump with the health label no_flow. We will pull a pump wire live in the demo.

## 36. Network outage with the pump running  ·  1:50

This is the requirement the brief cares most about, tested on the final firmware on the fifth of October with the pump running at the moment of the cut. We dropped all of the device's traffic at the laptop firewall for 120 seconds, while the broker and the backend stayed up and connected to each other. The grey band is the outage. The tank was at nine percent. In orange you see what the ESP32 recorded while it was offline: it kept filling, from nine to fifty one percent, with no pump transition at all, and these samples were buffered and replayed in order after the link returned, 125 of them. The grey dashed stretch after the outage is the blind zone of the sensor: readings are rejected and the model bridges the gap. Then the device stopped the pump by itself at 70.7 percent. Four messages out of 129 were lost, and we want to explain them rather than hide them: they were written into a socket the device still believed was open. The buffer only protects samples generated after the firmware decides the link is down. The first firmware waited for the fifteen second keep alive and lost ten; the final firmware closes a socket that has refused data for three seconds, so the loss fell to four. The control loop never stalled longer than 0.88 seconds, against up to ten seconds before the fix. The broker published the last will after 21.8 seconds, one and a half keep alive intervals as the MQTT specification allows, and the device was back online ten seconds after the restore.

## 39. Bugs found on the bench  ·  2:10

Four bugs taught us the most; all four are in Appendix A of the report. Each was invisible in the code and was found by counting something. First, an inverted relay: the configuration assumed the module was active low, it was active high, so every stop command started the pump while the telemetry said off. We proved the real state from the ripple of the motor current, twenty-seven millivolts when running versus four and a half when stopped. Second, the pump started after every reboot. At startup the firmware set the last valid reading time to now, so the sensor timeout counts from power up, but the validity flag was derived from that same timestamp, so for a few seconds the level was valid at zero percent and the controller saw an empty tank. We found it because automatic starts at 38 to 51 percent lined up with the sequence number restarting at one. Now the device stays in BOOT until a first reading is accepted. On the fifth of October the final firmware restarted with the tank at thirty three percent: the first level it published was thirty two, never zero, and the pump stayed off until the level drained below thirty. We will repeat this live. Third, a repeatable echo that is not the water: in the calibration, with the surface 7.9 to 8.7 centimetres from the sensor, all forty pings gave the same wrong value. A rule we had added, that a value repeated several times must be real, adopted it as an empty tank and started the pump every forty seconds. Consistency proves a reflector, not the right one; the rule is gone. Fourth, silent truncation: a telemetry message outgrew its buffer, the JSON library cut it without an error, and the MQTT library swallowed the parse exception. Everything looked alive, and nothing was stored.

## 40. Limitations and next steps  ·  1:30

We think pointing precisely at what falls short is as valuable as showing what works; this is Section 5.7 of the report. The main open item is flow measurement, highlighted. The hardware is fitted and the inlet now counts water and is calibrated, but we have validated it on one calibration run, so control and the volume counter still use flow from the level. The next step is to validate it over complete fills and move the volume counter to it, and to replace the outlet sensor with one rated down to 0.05 litres per minute. Second, the current scale factor: we read 471 to 487 milliamps for a pump rated 100 to 200, so either the divider or the sensor variant differs from our assumption; one multimeter reading settles it, and the present-or-absent use of current is unaffected. Third, the no current rule has not been triggered by a physical injection; we will pull a pump wire in the demo. Fourth, the ultrasonic blind zone: foam around the transducer, a stilling tube or a higher bracket would remove the stray echo. The rolling baseline needs several days to converge. And for a real deployment: MQTTS on port 8883, because the lab broker is not encrypted, a named tunnel, per-user accounts and over-the-air updates.

## 41. Conclusion  ·  1:40

To conclude, three ideas. First, we put the whole control law and every safety rule on the ESP32, following the rule that anything needing an immediate safe stop belongs at the edge; as a result the outage requirement is a property of the architecture: in our final test the tank kept filling through two minutes without network and stopped itself at 70.7 percent. Second, safety is structural: every path that can start the pump goes through one guard function, and overflow is prevented by independent layers, several of which do not depend on the filtered level at all. Third, we trusted measurements over datasheets and over our own expectations: the pump delivers a fifth of its rated flow and not even that consistently, the flow sensors counted interference until we rebuilt the power stage, the level sensor has a blind zone that only a stepwise calibration revealed, and our own data exposed a bug that started the pump after every reboot. On the bench, every automatic fill of the control session stopped between 70.1 and 70.8 percent, no false alarm was raised, the level reads within 0.68 centimetres of a ruler outside the blind zone, and commands are confirmed in 29 milliseconds. The open items are stated, not hidden: the flow sensors are partly validated, the no current rule was not injected, and the lab MQTT path is not encrypted. Now let us show you the real tank.

## Appendix: likely questions and short answers

**Is the flow sensor requirement really met?** Not simply "met". Both YF-S401 sensors are fitted, pulled up, read by interrupt and published. Until 22 September the pump injected about 1500 Hz of conducted interference; after the 5 October rewiring the inlet sensor counts real water (0 Hz off, 20 to 30 Hz on) and we calibrated it against the ruler, K = 85.5. It now feeds the tank model. But control and the volume counter still use flow derived from the level, because the turbine is validated on one calibration run, and the outlet flow is below the sensor's 0.3 L/min start. So: implemented, partly validated, limitation stated.

**Was NO_CURRENT tested?** Not by physical injection; its 2 s is a design value. We measured the signal it depends on: 471 to 487 mA with the relay closed, 0 mA open, about eleven times the noise floor. We pull a pump wire in the demo.

**Is the system secured with TLS?** No. Inside the lab the broker runs on plain port 1883 with passwords and an ACL; the public tunnel carries only the dashboard over HTTPS. MQTTS on 8883 is the deployment step. We do not claim end-to-end TLS.

**The pump started by itself after a reboot once. Is that fixed?** Yes. The device stays in BOOT until a first reading is accepted. On 5 October it restarted at 33 %: the first level published was 32 %, never zero, and the pump stayed off. We repeat it live.

**Why did the outage test lose 4 messages?** The ring buffer only protects samples produced after the firmware decides the link is down. The final firmware closes a socket that refuses data for 3 s, so about 3 to 4 samples are lost; the first firmware waited for the 15 s keep alive and lost 10.

**Why a relay and not a MOSFET?** The pump has two states and switches a few times per hour, so the MOSFET's switching speed brings nothing; the relay module drives the load from its own supply. A 1N4007 flyback diode protects it.

**Why SQLite and not a time series database?** We write one row per second from one node, three orders of magnitude below where relational engines struggle. We still follow the time series schema rule: only the timestamp is indexed.

**Why polling and not WebSocket?** The device publishes at 1 Hz, so polling at 1 Hz wastes nothing, and a WebSocket adds a stateful channel with its own failure modes. Beyond about ten nodes or 5 Hz we would switch.

**What if the ESP32 itself hangs?** The relay is driven off as the first instruction after any reset, so a watchdog reset stops the pump. A hardware float cut-off in series with the pump would be the next step for a real installation.

**Why is the sensor timeout 90 seconds?** Crossing the ultrasonic blind zone takes up to 75 s during a fill, and the tank model bridges it; with 4 s every fill stopped early, and 45 s still stopped fills inside the blind zone. During the wait the float, the raw distance guard and the run limits still protect the tank.
