Conclusion

1

Control and safety live on the edge, so the outage requirement follows from the architecture.

2

Safety is structural: one guard function and independent overflow layers.

3

Measurements over datasheets: every surprise was found by counting.

To conclude, three ideas. First, we put the whole control law and every safety rule on the ESP32, following the rule that anything needing an immediate safe stop belongs at the edge; as a result the outage requirement is a property of the architecture: in our final test the tank kept filling through two minutes without network and stopped itself at 70.7 percent. Second, safety is structural: every path that can start the pump goes through one guard function, and overflow is prevented by independent layers, several of which do not depend on the filtered level at all. Third, we trusted measurements over datasheets and over our own expectations: the pump delivers a fifth of its rated flow and not even that consistently, the flow sensors counted interference until we rebuilt the power stage, the level sensor has a blind zone that only a stepwise calibration revealed, and our own data exposed a bug that started the pump after every reboot. On the bench, every automatic fill of the control session stopped between 70.1 and 70.8 percent, no false alarm was raised, the level reads within 0.68 centimetres of a ruler outside the blind zone, and commands are confirmed in 29 milliseconds. The open items are stated, not hidden: the flow sensors are partly validated, the no current rule was not injected, and the lab MQTT path is not encrypted. Now let us show you the real tank.
